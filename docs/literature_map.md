# Literature map

This map organizes prior work by the exact component of the paper it can support. It is not a claim that prior papers establish the full competence-rent framework.

## 1. Superstar economics

### Rosen (1981), The Economics of Superstars

Contribution: explains why small differences in talent can generate large differences in rewards when top producers can serve very large markets.

Use here: theoretical foundation for the scaling side of market integration.

Does not establish: that digital connectivity caused disappearance of local occupational niches.

## 2. Tradability of services

### Gervais and Jensen (2019), The Tradability of Services: Geographic Concentration and Trade Costs

Contribution: estimates trade costs across a large set of service and manufacturing industries and shows substantial heterogeneity in service tradability.

Use here: supports the premise that many services can face geographic competition and that services cannot simply be treated as non-tradable.

Potential methodological use: candidate external validation for our occupation or industry tradability construction.

Does not establish: a digital-treatment effect on local competence rents.

## 3. Local labor-market adjustment to market integration

### Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013), The China Syndrome

Contribution: demonstrates that exposure to external competition can generate persistent, geographically concentrated labor-market effects.

Use here: establishes a closely related empirical tradition for studying local adjustment to expanded competition.

Analogy only: import competition in manufacturing is not the same treatment as digital integration of services.

## 4. Superstar firms and concentration

### Autor, Dorn, Katz, Patterson, and Van Reenen (2020), The Fall of the Labor Share and the Rise of Superstar Firms

Contribution: links industry concentration and the rise of high-productivity firms to declining labor shares.

Use here: adjacent evidence that scalable technologies and market-share reallocation can concentrate economic activity.

Do not conflate firm concentration with creator-level or occupation-level concentration.

## 5. Platform attention and creator earnings

### Strauss, Yang, and Mazzucato (2026), Rich-get-richer? Platform Attention and Earnings Inequality Using Patreon Earnings Data

Contribution: finds heavy-tailed creator earnings and reports that platforms with greater earnings concentration have lower mean and median creator earnings and a weaker creator middle class.

Use here: directly relevant to the claim that more open creator entry can coexist with a weak middle of the earnings distribution.

Important limitation: Patreon earnings are not total creator earnings, and the evidence should not be generalized mechanically to all creators or all platforms.

## 6. Generative AI and productivity

### Noy and Zhang (2023)

Contribution: randomized evidence on professional writing tasks showing faster completion and higher quality with generative AI, with larger gains among initially weaker performers.

Use here: causal mechanism evidence that AI can compress measured productivity differences in a particular class of cognitive tasks.

Do not infer: aggregate employment effects.

### Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025)

Contribution: workplace evidence from customer support showing productivity gains with larger benefits for less experienced workers.

Use here: mechanism evidence for skill compression and diffusion of best practices.

Do not infer: universal effects across occupations.

## 7. Generative AI and online labor demand

### Demirci, Hannane, and Zhu (2025)

Contribution: documents a large decline in postings for automation-prone writing and coding work relative to manual-intensive categories after ChatGPT, and a decline in image-creation postings after diffusion of image-generation tools.

Use here: early market evidence in a setting already highly digitally tradable.

Causal language must match the paper's actual identification strategy rather than relying on the publication venue or magnitude alone.

## 8. Measurement and design literature still to add

Priority additions:

- historical broadband rollout and quasi-experimental broadband papers
- remote-work feasibility and occupational task indices
- occupation harmonization methods
- modern staggered difference-in-differences
- sensitivity to parallel-trend violations
- spatial inference
- market access and agglomeration
- local news decline and civic/economic spillovers
- occupational entry and career-choice response to changing returns
- search, recommendation, and attention concentration

## 9. Candidate contribution relative to literature

The provisional contribution is not that any single mechanism is unknown.

The candidate contribution is to unify and empirically test a sequence:

1. geography creates local scarcity rents for competence
2. digital connectivity lowers the protection supplied by geography
3. scalable digital markets can concentrate attention and rewards
4. generative AI may reduce the scarcity of competent output itself
5. the economically important margin may be the middle of the competence distribution and the disappearance of local occupational niches

Novelty will be claimed only after a systematic search establishes that this sequence and the proposed empirical test are not already present in substantially equivalent form.
